Canonly/The Compliance-Approved AEO Playbook

The Compliance-Approved AEO Playbook is the operating manual for winning AI citations when every sentence needs legal review.

The Compliance-Approved AEO Playbook explains how regulated brands earn AI citations without loosening review. It covers the operating model (legal approves claims once, marketing composes freely), building a claim library from assets already cleared, the answer-block architecture engines cite, the review workflow legal accepts, two-meter measurement, and vertical annexes for pharma, banking, wealth and insurance.

By Jason Garrett, founder of Canonly and Left Coast Agency / Coast Studio · Updated October 16, 2026

The playbook is ten chapters plus a template appendix. The cover promises AEO because that is what regulated marketers search for. The system inside is larger: an operating model where legal approves content once and marketing composes freely, with AI search as the first channel it unlocks. The chapter summaries below are the public edition. The full PDF, with templates and vertical annexes, is available through the form at the bottom of this page.

Chapter 1: What changed on the front page?

Buyers moved to AI answers quietly and all at once. Adobe Analytics measured generative-AI referral traffic to US sites growing more than tenfold between July 2024 and February 2025, and twelvefold for banking. The most regulated audiences adopted fastest. The chapter lays out the invisibility ledger: 22 of the top 75 US banks under 0.3% citation share and the three largest bank-owned domains at 6.8% (The Financial Brand, 2026), and roughly a quarter of pharma citations concentrated in two companies (5W, Q2 2026). The winners are the brands whose information exists in the fresh, structured, quotable form engines prefer. Content operations transfer to the new channel.

Chapter 2: Why do regulated content operations fail at AEO?

The review queue was built for quarterly campaigns. It reviews every asset from scratch, in three or more rounds, at one to three weeks per round. Answer engines reward content that is question-shaped, specific, entity-rich and fresh. Forty answer pages per product line through that queue is a year of review capacity. Hiring more reviewers buys linear capacity against an exponential need. Loosening the standard trades a visibility problem for an enforcement problem in a year when FDA announced roughly 100 cease-and-desist letters on DTC drug advertising and FINRA put AI-generated communications squarely inside Rule 2210. The only way through is to change what gets reviewed.

Chapter 3: What is the review-once, compose-freely operating model?

Contract teams solved this a decade ago with clause libraries and composition rules, and Ironclad turned it into software. The chapter defines the three primitives (approved claims, governed blocks, composition rules), what changes for legal (approve components once, review exceptions, inherit an automatic audit trail) and what changes for marketing (compose freely inside visible lines, let AI draft inside the system). It spells out exception-only review concretely: what still routes to legal and what ships with no review cycle.

Chapter 4: How do you build a pre-approved claim library from assets legal already cleared?

Harvest what legal already approved. Every permitted claim already exists in prescribing information, cleared brochures, regulated disclosures or the last campaign that passed review. The chapter runs the claim inventory workshop (one to two days per product line), defines the anatomy of a library entry (approved wording and variants, substantiation, approved contexts and required pairings, prohibited contexts, expiry and owner) and sets the governance that keeps the library current: named owners, versioning, and automatic expiry.

Chapter 5: What answer-block architecture do engines cite?

Four properties drive citation: the page answers the literal question, the answer is specific, the page is machine-legible, and the content is fresh. One block answers one question, states it verbatim, answers in the first sentence, carries supporting claims with their pairings, and renders a structured-data twin as FAQPage JSON-LD generated from the same source. The technical layer is brief: generated JSON-LD, an llms.txt index at the domain root, and crawlable server-rendered HTML with no PDF-only answers. The chapter closes on the owned-site ceiling and why blocks that travel to earned and partner surfaces are the second payoff.

Chapter 6: What review workflow will legal accept?

Legal designs the system and owns the claim library. Three roles cover the workflow: claim owners (legal, MLR, compliance), composers (marketing) and exception reviewers (legal). Service levels are set by exception type. The audit trail has four non-negotiable properties: append-only, complete, versioned and exportable as a per-asset evidence packet. Roll out with one product line, one claim inventory and one motivated attorney, and let the first cycle's numbers recruit the rest.

Chapter 7: How do you measure with two meters?

Citation share justifies the program; review velocity proves the operating model within a month. The chapter specifies the prompt panel (about twelve real buyer questions per product line), repeated sampling reported as a citation rate, a two-week baseline before publishing, and the velocity trio: review rounds per asset, days from draft to approval, and percent of content shipped with zero review cycles. Both meters go on one page, monthly, to marketing and legal leadership together.

Chapter 8: What do the vertical annexes cover?

  • Pharma and health. OPDP and MLR: label-scoped claims, fair balance, ISI pairing rules, Form FDA 2253 at first use, expiry on label change. See MLR review and AI search.
  • Banking and fintech. UDAAP and FTC Act Section 5: rate and fee disclosures as required pairings, substantiation for comparative claims, prohibited-context rules for eligibility language.
  • Wealth and advisory. SEC Marketing Rule and FINRA Rule 2210: principal approval per block, performance and testimonial pairings, filing and recordkeeping. See FINRA 2210 in AI answers.
  • Insurance. State advertising regulations modeled on the NAIC advertising model rules: state-scoped approved contexts, required identification of the insurer, and expiry tied to form and rate filings.

Chapter 9: What does the 90-day implementation plan look like?

Week by week: claim inventory, first twenty blocks, governed composition, first published answer pages, first measurement cycle. The chapter includes staffing and effort estimates for a single product line pilot.

Chapter 10: What are the objections and edge cases?

"Our legal team will never agree." "AI engines don't cite brand sites anyway." "What about social and field content?" "What if the rules change?" Each gets a direct answer, including the honest version of the earned-media question.

Appendix: Which templates are included?

Claim library schema, answer block specification, prompt panel worksheet, review SLA by exception type, and evidence packet checklist.

Frequently asked questions

Who is the Compliance-Approved AEO Playbook for?

Heads of digital, content and growth at pharma, health, banking, insurance and wealth brands, and the legal, MLR and compliance partners who review their work. If marketing copy routes through a review queue, it was written for that team.

Is the playbook generic AEO advice?

No. It starts from the review queue as a fixed constraint and builds the operating model, claim library, answer-block architecture, legal workflow and measurement plan around it, with annexes for each regulated vertical.
